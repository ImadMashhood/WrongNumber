The current gameplay loop relies heavily on receiving an emergency call, questioning the caller, determining whether the emergency is genuine, and dispatching appropriate resources.
Although this works as a dispatch simulator, it does not consistently create the conversational tension, deception, manipulation, or unexpected situations needed for a compelling multiplayer party game.
The objective of this overhaul is to make AI callers feel like independent characters with their own motivations, secrets, fears, personalities, and agendas.
The core gameplay should shift from "Is this emergency real?" to "What is this person actually trying to accomplish, and can I figure it out before making a decision?"
The player should experience:
- Callers who deliberately lie and maintain believable cover stories.
- Callers who conceal information for understandable reasons.
- Callers who manipulate the dispatcher into taking particular actions.
- Callers who become angry, defensive, cooperative, frightened, sarcastic, or evasive based on player behavior.
- Callers who contradict themselves and can be confronted with evidence.
- Callers who can be persuaded to reveal secrets.
- Callers who are genuinely confused rather than malicious.
- Callers who attempt to manipulate multiple dispatchers differently.
- Callers who remember previous conversations and decisions.
- Situations in which dispatching immediately is appropriate, even when information is incomplete.
The intended experience is a mixture of conversational investigation, psychological deduction, emergency management, and chaotic comedy.
The game must remain unpredictable without becoming unfair or mechanically arbitrary.
The existing repository already contains substantial systems that should be reused rather than rewritten.

Inspect the actual code and current interfaces before making changes. Documentation may not fully reflect the latest implementation.
Preserve the existing design principles:
1. The simulation owns incident truth.
2. AI output cannot arbitrarily create incidents or change established facts.
3. Multiplayer remains server-authoritative.
4. AI provider credentials remain outside the game client.
5. AI provider failures terminate conversations according to existing requirements; do not introduce a scripted fallback.
6. Dialogue remains natural and unrestricted by predetermined question menus.
7. Existing city consequences, responder pathfinding, and dispatch controls remain functional.
This overhaul should primarily add a richer private character model, conversational state, structured deception mechanics, and investigation outcomes.
Each incoming call must have two distinct layers.
This represents objective reality.
Examples:
- A building is genuinely on fire.
- A man is falsely accusing his neighbor.
- A robbery occurred, but the caller committed it.
- A woman is experiencing a medical emergency.
- Someone mistakenly believes an intruder entered their home.
- A caller wants police to remove someone from a property.
This truth must be generated or selected before the conversation begins.
This represents what the caller wants, believes, fears, and is willing to do.
For example, a caller reporting a robbery might actually want to:
- Receive police protection.
- Conceal their own involvement.
- Obtain an official incident report.
- Have another person arrested.
- Distract officers from another location.
- Avoid admitting an embarrassing mistake.
Two callers reporting identical incidents may behave completely differently because their agendas differ.
The caller's desired outcome must influence dialogue and behavior, not merely appear as background text.
Introduce an internal CallerPsychology or equivalent data model.
Suggested conceptual structure:

struct CallerPsychology {

    trust: f32,

    deception_strategy: DeceptionStrategy,
    disclosure_rules: Vec<DisclosureRule>,

This is illustrative. Adapt to existing types and conventions.
Personality should remain relatively stable throughout a call.
Potential dimensions:
- Honesty
- Patience
- Aggressiveness
- Confidence
- Anxiety
- Manipulativeness
- Sociability
- Impulsiveness
- Distrust of authority
Emotional state should change during conversation.
Examples:
- Calm
- Nervous
- Frustrated
- Angry
- Panicked
- Defensive
- Embarrassed
- Relieved
- Cooperative
- Suspicious
Emotional changes must be caused by meaningful conversational events.
A naturally anxious caller should not become calm simply because the dispatcher asks one polite question.
Likewise, an aggressive caller should not automatically become cooperative after receiving an apology.
Emotional changes should affect word choice, willingness to disclose information, interruptions, and conversational tactics.
Emotions are not reliable proof of deception. Genuine victims may behave aggressively or inconsistently, and deceptive callers may remain calm.
Not every caller should be deceptive.
Introduce distinct caller truthfulness categories.
The caller accurately describes the emergency to the best of their knowledge.
The challenge is triage, not interrogation.
The caller sincerely believes something false.
Example: Someone believes a neighbor is stealing their packages, but the deliveries were made to the wrong address.
They are not intentionally lying.
The caller reports genuine events but intentionally omits important information.
Example: A man reports being attacked but refuses to admit he initiated the confrontation.
The caller invents or distorts information to obtain a desired outcome.
Example: A caller falsely reports an armed suspect to get police sent to a rival's address.
The caller wants help but fears the consequences of revealing the truth.
Example: Someone reports a medical emergency but conceals drug use because they fear legal trouble.
The caller attempts to influence the dispatcher using emotional pressure, flattery, threats, false urgency, or appeals to authority.
Manipulation can coexist with truthful or deceptive reports.
Important: These categories are not necessarily mutually exclusive. A caller can be truthful about an emergency while manipulating the dispatcher to prioritize them.
A major failure mode of LLM-driven deduction games is unlimited improvisation.
If a player catches a contradiction, an unrestricted LLM may invent a new explanation that retroactively repairs the story.
This makes deduction meaningless.
Implement a fixed scenario truth and a separate, constrained cover story.
Example:
Actual truth
- Caller is angry at a neighbor.
- Neighbor does not possess a weapon.
- Caller wants police to remove the neighbor.
- Caller has never entered the neighbor's apartment.
Cover story
- Caller claims to have seen a gun.
- Caller claims the neighbor threatened someone.
- Caller says they observed the situation from outside.
- Caller does not know the interior layout of the apartment.
Potential contradiction
The caller claims to have seen something that could not reasonably be visible from the claimed observation point.
The AI should be allowed to:
- Evade the question.
- Become defensive.
- Refuse to answer.
- Repeat an earlier claim.
- Admit uncertainty.
- Change its story if the scenario explicitly permits that behavior.
- Confess when appropriate conditions are met.
It should not freely invent new physical evidence, witnesses, or facts that invalidate the scenario's intended deduction.
Each deceptive scenario should define:
1. Immutable actual facts.
2. Facts the caller knows.
3. Facts the caller falsely believes.
4. Deliberately fabricated claims.
5. Information the caller intends to hide.
6. Allowed evasions or alternate explanations.
7. Evidence that could undermine the cover story.
8. Conditions under which the caller may admit the truth.
The LLM should generate natural dialogue within these constraints.
Do not assume prompt instructions alone guarantee consistency.
The player must be able to investigate naturally.
There should be no requirement to select predetermined dialogue options.
Example interactions:
Dispatcher: "Where exactly were you standing when you saw the gun?"
Caller: "Across the street. I already told you."
Dispatcher: "Then how did you see inside the bedroom?"
Caller: "I don't know, man. The curtains were open."
Dispatcher: "You said it happened in the kitchen earlier."
Caller: "Look, are you sending somebody or not?"
The system should recognize that the player is challenging an inconsistency.
It should distinguish between:
- Asking for information.
- Requesting clarification.
- Presenting evidence.
- Challenging a contradiction.
- Accusing the caller of lying.
- Reassuring the caller.
- Negotiating cooperation.
- Applying pressure.
- Threatening consequences.
- Attempting to trick the caller.
- Making irrelevant remarks.
- Insulting or provoking the caller.
These conversational actions should influence the caller's psychological state and disclosure decisions.
A player's exact wording should matter where appropriate, but the game must not depend on specific magic phrases.
Introduce a structured investigation state for each call.
Potential evidence types:
- Caller statements
- Timeline inconsistencies
- Conflicting locations
- Physical impossibilities
- Contradictory descriptions
- External dispatch records
- Prior caller history
- Witness reports
- Verified information
- Caller admissions
Evidence should have provenance.
For example:
```text
Statement #12
Caller: "I saw him enter at 9 PM."

Statement #18
Caller: "I was at work until 11 PM."

Potential contradiction:
Caller claims direct observation while allegedly elsewhere.
```
However, the system must distinguish between a potential contradiction and a verified contradiction.
An LLM may propose that two statements conflict, but the simulation should only award a confirmed contradiction when supported by structured scenario facts or validated evidence.
Do not reward players merely for repeatedly accusing callers of lying.
Players should be able to verbally confront a caller.
Successful confrontation may cause:
- A partial admission.
- A full confession.
- A revised account.
- Increased defensiveness.
- A refusal to cooperate.
- A hang-up.
- Disclosure of another hidden fact.
The result should depend on the scenario, caller psychology, available evidence, and previous interaction.
A confession should be a possible outcome, not a guaranteed reward for asking enough questions.
Implement a lightweight, deterministic state transition system.
For example:
```text
Initial state:
Trust: 45
Stress: 30
Defensiveness: 40
Cooperation: 55

Dispatcher reassures caller:
Trust increases slightly.
Stress may decrease.

Dispatcher presents strong contradictory evidence:
Stress increases.
Defensiveness may increase or decrease.
Confession becomes possible.

Dispatcher repeatedly insults caller:
Trust decreases.
Cooperation decreases.
Caller may hang up.
```
The specific numbers are illustrative and should be tuned through playtesting.
The LLM should not have unrestricted authority to modify these values.
Instead, use structured conversational events validated by the host.
A possible pipeline:
```text
Player speaks
    ↓
Speech-to-text / text input
    ↓
Conversation interpretation
    ↓
Validated conversational event
    ↓
Host updates psychological state
    ↓
Disclosure rules evaluated
    ↓
LLM generates in-character response
    ↓
Output validation
    ↓
Player hears response
```
Avoid requiring multiple paid LLM calls for every player utterance.
Prefer a single structured provider response containing both natural dialogue and proposed conversational metadata, followed by deterministic validation.
Use the existing provider-neutral caller architecture.
Callers must not behave like cooperative information databases.
A caller may:
- Demand immediate action.
- Refuse to provide their name.
- Refuse to identify another person.
- Question the dispatcher's competence.
- Demand a supervisor.
- Threaten to complain.
- Ask for reassurance.
- Refuse to answer incriminating questions.
- Attempt to change the subject.
- Offer a misleading explanation.
- Demand confidentiality.
- Hang up when pushed too far.
However, caller resistance must have understandable motivations.
A terrified victim refusing to cooperate should feel different from a criminal trying to misdirect police.
Certain information may be revealed only after the player establishes sufficient trust or satisfies a scenario-specific condition.
Example:
A caller refuses to explain why someone is unconscious.
The dispatcher might say:
"Listen, I'm not here to judge you. I need to know what happened so the ambulance knows what to expect."
A successful reassurance could cause the caller to admit that drugs were involved.
The admission provides useful medical information and improves the quality of the response.
The player should feel that their conversational approach changed the outcome.
The game supports up to six players.
Use this existing multiplayer capability to create arguments and social interaction naturally.
A caller should retain their conversational history when transferred.
Example:
Dispatcher A spends two minutes questioning a caller.
The caller becomes angry and refuses to cooperate.
Dispatcher B takes over and attempts a calmer approach.
The caller remembers that Dispatcher A accused them of lying.
This can create genuine player-to-player conflict.
Players should be able to disagree over how to handle a caller.
One player may want to dispatch immediately.
Another may believe the caller is manipulating the department.
Another may want to continue investigating.
These differences should have consequences.
Players should be able to review the case transcript and discovered evidence.
They should not automatically receive the caller's hidden motives or actual incident truth.
Some incidents may involve multiple callers describing the same underlying event.
Example:
- Caller A reports an armed robbery.
- Caller B claims the supposed victim was actually the aggressor.
- Caller C reports hearing gunshots but did not see anything.
Different dispatchers investigate different accounts.
The team must compare information and decide what to believe.
This is particularly valuable for multiplayer because it creates genuine disagreements based on incomplete information.
The underlying event must remain consistent across all connected calls.
Introduce persistent in-session caller memory.
Callers should remember:
- Previous conversations.
- Earlier dispatch decisions.
- Promises made by dispatchers.
- Whether they were accused of lying.
- Whether emergency responders were sent.
- Relevant prior incidents.
- Their relationship with the department.
Example:
A player refuses a caller's earlier report.
Later, the same caller phones again:
"Oh, it's you again. Last time you told me I was wasting your time."
The caller may be less cooperative.
Another caller might become more trusting after receiving appropriate help.
Memory should be stored as structured events and concise summaries rather than unlimited transcripts.
Caller identity and memory must remain consistent across the session.
Do not allow an AI-generated recollection to invent events that never happened.

Extend the current complaint catalogue rather than replacing its existing entries wholesale.
Personal disputes
Neighbor conflicts, relationship arguments, property disagreements, workplace conflicts, family disputes.
Criminal deception
False accusations, attempts to redirect police, concealment of involvement, fabricated victimhood.
Embarrassing emergencies
Callers concealing personal circumstances, illegal activity, or socially embarrassing information.
Mistaken emergencies
Misinterpreted noises, misunderstandings, incorrect addresses, false assumptions.

Callers who are panicked, confused, injured, incoherent, or unable to cooperate.
Absurd comedy
Ridiculous complaints, bizarre demands, stubborn callers, misunderstandings, and escalating interpersonal arguments.
Comedy should emerge from character behavior and circumstances rather than every caller behaving like a joke character.
Separate three concerns.
Owned by the Rust simulation.
Contains actual truth, evidence, incident state, deadlines, and dispatch consequences.
Never expose the entire authoritative scenario to joining clients.
Contains the information the AI character may know or believe.
This includes:
- Personal background
- Goals
- Cover story
- Allowed lies
- Emotional state
- Known facts
- Hidden information
- Recent conversation history
- Permitted disclosures
The AI may receive this limited private context.
It should not receive unrelated future events, global scoring details, or authoritative information outside the character's knowledge.
The AI produces a conversational response consistent with the caller's private knowledge and current psychological state.
The response should sound natural, not like a game NPC reading structured metadata.
